\significancestatement{Marine species are widely expected to respond to ocean warming through predictable poleward and deeper redistributions. Using data from more than 200 fish populations across two ocean basins, we show that this foundational expectation is not reflected in the net movement of marine communities. While individual populations shift, their movements are multidirectional and largely cancel each other out, resulting in near-zero net displacement when averaged across regions. In contrast, the average temperature within species’ habitats has risen significantly, indicating that spatial shifts are not keeping pace with ocean warming. These findings suggest that monitoring geographic range shifts alone may underestimate the true thermal stress experienced by marine ecosystems under climate change.}

\begin{abstract}
Poleward and deeper range shifts are among the most frequently reported biological responses to ocean warming, and their rates are used as indicators of climate impact. We examined 226 demersal fish populations from 17 standardized bottom-trawl surveys across 11 regions of the North Atlantic and Northeast Pacific, sampled between 1994 and 2021, estimating trends in latitude, longitude, depth and occupied bottom temperature within a single hierarchical framework. Individual populations moved a great deal, at a median absolute rate of 13.3 km per decade in latitude and 1.2 m per decade in depth. Yet these movements showed no consistent overall direction: cross-regional trends in latitude, longitude and depth were all close to zero. Directionally consistent shifts did emerge within some regions, where populations that moved poleward or deeper warmed less than those that stayed or moved in the opposite direction. The temperature populations occupied nonetheless rose by 0.18$^\circ$C per decade on average (95\% CI:
0.06, 0.29), and closely followed local warming rates in each region. Marine fishes are moving, but in ways that do not add up to net movement of marine communities and that do little to reduce their exposure to warming. Monitoring range shifts alone may therefore underestimate the thermal stress marine species experience under climate change.
\end{abstract}

The world's oceans are warming rapidly, driving a major reorganization of marine biodiversity with consequences for ecosystems and human use \citep{ipcc_ocean_2022, pecl_biodiversity_2017}. To stay within the temperatures that support growth, survival and reproduction, marine ectotherms are expected to move poleward or into deeper water \citep{sundayThermalToleranceGlobal2012}. Studies across taxa and regions report such movements so consistently that range shifts have become the signature biological response to climate change \citep{parmesan_globally_2003, parmesan_ecological_2006, poloczanska_global_2013, poloczanska_responses_2016}. Rates of redistribution are now widely used to quantify biological impacts of warming, inform global climate assessments \citep{ipcc_ocean_2022, poloczanska_responses_2016}, and anticipate fisheries conflicts as stocks cross management boundaries \citep{pinsky_preparing_2018, palacios-abrantes_climate_2025}.
 
Those rates rest on a narrower foundation than their reach implies. Most derive from syntheses of published studies whose underlying analyses vary widely in spatial extent, duration, taxonomic coverage, sampling intensity, and statistical methods---differences that influence both the magnitude and even the direction of estimated shifts \citep{poloczanska_global_2013, brown_ecological_2016, lenoir_species_2020, lawlor_mechanisms_2024}. Individual studies typically estimate distributional change directly from survey observations, making inferred shifts sensitive to sampling variability, survey redesign, and interannual fluctuations \citep{thorson_model-based_2016}. They also tend to examine redistribution along a single spatial dimension---usually latitude and, less often, depth \citep{lenoir_climate-related_2015}---while significant shifts are more likely than null results to enter the published literature.

These limitations matter because marine responses to warming are far from uniform. Some populations shift poleward or deeper, others remain stationary despite sustained warming, and still others move in unexpected directions, sometimes within the same region \citep{rubenstein_climate_2023, fuchs_wrong-way_2020, lawlor_mechanisms_2024}. Averaging heterogeneous studies of heterogeneous responses therefore provides only limited insight into whether coherent large-scale patterns of redistribution emerge. Expectations about how marine species redistribute under warming rest on the premise that distributions closely follow temperature \citep{sundayThermalToleranceGlobal2012, burrows_pace_2011}. Yet how far species actually move has rarely been estimated from standardized observations spanning multiple ocean basins \Parasplit and analysed within a common framework that propagates uncertainty in estimated shift.



Here we analyse 226 demersal (bottom-living) fish populations from 17 standardized scientific bottom-trawl surveys across 11 regions of the North Atlantic and Northeast Pacific, sampled between 1994 and 2021. These regions span wide latitudinal and climatic gradients and provide some of the most comprehensive long-term records of marine biodiversity change \citep{maureaud_are_2021, maureaud_fishglob_data_2024}. Warming over this period varied widely among them, from some of the fastest-warming continental shelf seas on Earth \citep{pershing_slow_2015, song_arctic_2023} to regions with no detectable trend (Fig.~\ref{fig:map}). 

Marine species can respond to warming in multiple ways, including shifts in latitude or longitude, movement into deeper water, or remaining in place while conditions warm around them, and these may occur in combination \citep{nye_changing_2009}. For each population, we therefore fit a spatiotemporal species distribution model and extract four annual descriptors of its distribution: the latitude and longitude of its biomass centroid, its depth centroid, and its mean occupied bottom temperature. A hierarchical Bayesian model then estimates long-term trends in all four, propagating uncertainty from the distribution models, allowing trends in different dimensions to covary among species within a region, and separating species- and region-level variation from shared temporal trends. By combining standardized observations with this hierarchical framework, we estimate rates of distributional change for individual populations, test whether these movements share a direction within regions and combine into a consistent signal across ocean basins, assess whether redistribution has moderated thermal exposure, and evaluate whether any of these scale with regional rates of warming.

This lack of net spatial redistribution reflects opposing species responses, with similar proportions of species shifting in opposite directions when aggregated across regions (Fig.~\ref{fig:prop_significant}a). \subsection*{Weak regional trends, but coherent responses within some regions}

At the regional scale, posterior slopes for spatial shifts varied in direction and were generally small and uncertain, offering limited evidence for consistent changes in mean latitude, longitude, or depth within regions (Fig.~\ref{fig:posterior_slopes}b). Among spatial dimensions, depth showed the most consistent---though still modest---signals, with slight increases in mean depth along the U.S. West Coast (USWC; \DepthUSWCMedian{}~m~decade$^{-1}$ \DepthUSWCCI{}) and the Northeast U.S. and Scotian Shelf (NEUS–SS; \DepthNEUSMedian{}~m~decade$^{-1}$ \DepthNEUSCI{}). Although regional slopes were weak, many species within some regions shifted in the same direction (Fig.~\ref{fig:prop_significant}b). In the Eastern Bering Sea (EBS), 64\% of species moved northward, with a similar proportion in the North Sea (NS; 60\%), whereas 75\% of species in the Baltic Sea (BAL) shifted southward. Depth shifts were also directionally consistent within some regions, with many species deepening in the Northeast U.S. and Scotian Shelf (39\%) and British Columbia (BC; 31\%), while relatively few shifted shallower. Depth shifts were absent or rare in other regions, with no supported changes in the Northern Iberian Coast (NIC) and only 3\% of species shifting depth in the Celtic–Biscay Shelf (CBS).

To isolate the effect of redistribution on thermal exposure, we contrasted trends in mean occupied bottom temperature with warming expected under static species distributions. Across most regions, observed and static warming were similar, suggesting that redistribution had limited net influence on thermal exposure (Supplementary Fig. S1).

Within some regions, however, species shifting poleward or deeper experienced smaller increases in occupied temperature than species that remained stationary or shifted in the opposite direction (Supplementary Fig. S2). Negative correlations between latitudinal shifts and warming of occupied temperature were strongest in the Northeast U.S. and Scotian Shelf ($\rho$ = \rhocogycthermalnichecNEUS), Celtic–Biscay Shelf (\rhocogycthermalnichecCBS), North Sea (\rhocogycthermalnichecNS), and Eastern Bering Sea (\rhocogycthermalnichecEBS). Depth shifts showed similar patterns in the Gulf of Mexico (\rhodepthnichecthermalnichecGOM), British Columbia (\rhodepthnichecthermalnichecBC), and North Sea (\rhodepthnichecthermalnichecNS). By contrast, longitudinal shifts were generally weakly associated with change in occupied temperature, except in the Northeast U.S. and Scotian Shelf, where longitudinal movements co-varied with latitude (\rhocogyccogxcNEUS).

Spatial shifts along different axes also co-varied in other regions. For example, latitudinal and longitudinal shifts were strongly positively associated in the Gulf of Mexico, and negatively associated in British Columbia and the U.S. West Coast, reflecting coast-parallel movements. In the North Sea, latitudinal and depth shifts were correlated, with northward movements coinciding with deepening (\rhodepthnicheccogycNS).

\section*{Discussion}

Marine biodiversity is reorganizing under ocean warming, and studies across taxa and regions have documented widespread poleward and deeper shifts \citep{poloczanska_global_2013, dulvy_climate_2008, poloczanska_responses_2016, perry_climate_2005}. Across 226 populations, we found no consistent net redistribution in latitude, longitude or depth. This was not for want of movement. Individual populations moved roughly ten times further than the aggregate trends imply, at median absolute rates of 13.3~km~decade$^{-1}$ in latitude, 12.3~km~decade$^{-1}$ in longitude and 1.2~m~decade$^{-1}$ in depth, but they moved in opposing directions and largely cancelled one another, with coherence emerging only within some regions. At the same time, we observed widespread warming of the temperatures those populations occupied across most taxa and regions.

This widespread warming of occupied temperatures represents the most consistent signal across regions and taxa. It indicates that most species experienced increasing temperatures in the habitats they occupied, even where spatial redistribution occurred. This finding suggests that redistribution rarely provided full thermal compensation under ocean warming. A comparable analysis in the Northeast Pacific has likewise found that the temperatures occupied by marine populations change over time, with the extent of change varying among species \citep{ward_win_2024}. A comparison with warming expected under spatially fixed distributions further supports this interpretation: when averaged across species within regions, observed and static warming were similar in most systems, indicating limited net buffering of background ocean warming through redistribution. Only in a few regions, such as the Eastern Bering Sea, did mean observed warming fall well below static expectations, consistent with partial compensation at the regional scale.

Thermal compensation did nonetheless emerge within some regions, where species that shifted poleward or deeper tended to experience smaller increases in occupied temperature. This is evident in systems with strong latitudinal temperature gradients and few barriers to movement, such as the Northeast U.S. and Scotian Shelf, Celtic--Biscay Shelf, the North Sea and the Eastern Bering Sea, where poleward movements were associated with reduced warming of occupied temperatures. In other regions, depth-mediated responses appeared more important. For example, in the Gulf of Mexico---where poleward redistribution is geographically constrained---and in British Columbia, shifts to greater depths were associated with reduced warming of occupied temperatures. Because vertical temperature gradients are steep relative to latitude or longitude, modest changes in depth can offset substantial warming, and the pace at which isotherms migrate through the water column has recently been shown to add an important dimension to the movement expected of marine species \citep{gruenburgVerticalClimateVelocity2025}. The North Sea represents a particularly coherent case, with many species shifting in concert both poleward and deeper, reflecting the tightly coupled latitudinal and vertical thermal gradients in the area \citep{ices_atlas_north_sea_1993}. These well-documented examples, such as North Sea cod, illustrate that effective thermal compensation can occur under specific geographic and environmental configurations (Supplementary Data S1; \citealp{perry_climate_2005, engelhard_climate_2014}), but such cases remain the exception rather than the rule in our analysis.

Weak net spatial redistribution does not imply a lack of biological response to ocean warming. Rather, it emerges from the diversity of redistribution pathways available to marine species and the constraints that shape them. Although temperature gradients broadly align with latitude and depth, local bathymetry, coastlines, ocean circulation, and habitat structure generate region-specific pathways or obstacles for movement \citep{pinsky_marine_2013, garcia_molinos_climate_2016}. Furthermore, species traits, habitat associations, and non-thermal drivers such as fishing pressure, oxygen availability, and prey distributions may modulate these responses \citep{sunday_species_2015, deutsch_climate_2015, wisz_role_2013, bandara_importance_2024}. As a result, heterogeneous---and sometimes opposing---population-level movements may arise within and among regions under similar warming trends, producing weak aggregate signals at broader spatial scales, as also found for change in community composition across these surveys \citep{kitchelMarineCommunitiesNot2025}.

Differences in warming do not offer an alternative explanation. Trends in latitude, longitude and depth were similar in regions with and without a significant warming trend, and showed no detectable scaling with regional warming rate, although depth was the one dimension tending in the expected direction. Occupied temperature, by contrast, closely followed local warming. Variation in the strength of the climate signal therefore does not account for the heterogeneity we observe, suggesting that the constraints described above matter more than the amount of warming a region experienced. Movement was also not always aligned with local warming: in the Eastern Bering Sea, 64\% of species moved northward despite no detectable regional trend in bottom temperature, indicating that drivers other than temperature can shape distributional change in some systems.

The lack of strong net spatial redistribution contrasts with several cross-regional syntheses that report substantial, directional range shifts. For example, Poloczanska et al. \citep{poloczanska_global_2013} estimated mean poleward movements of roughly 30--70 km decade$^{-1}$ at species' centroids and leading edges, with comparable rates reported in the BioShifts database \citep{lenoir_species_2020}. By comparison, the cross-regional trends we estimate were one to two orders of magnitude smaller and lacked a consistent direction. Our analysis focuses on well-sampled species from standardized surveys, enabling consistent estimation of spatial and thermal metrics and explicit propagation of uncertainty across multiple dimensions of redistribution and thermal exposure. This design results in narrower taxonomic and geographic coverage than a global synthesis, but it allows redistribution pathways to be evaluated within a robust and unified statistical framework. Many cross-regional analyses pool heterogeneous datasets and rely on summary metrics such as range edges, often without explicitly accounting for uncertainty in underlying shifts \citep{brown_ecological_2016, lenoir_species_2020, dahms_temperature_2023}. Estimates of distributional change may also be sensitive to spatial variation in sampling intensity, survey coverage, and catchability if observation processes are not formally modelled \citep{thorson_model-based_2016}. By contrast, our two-step framework estimates spatial and thermal metrics directly from standardized survey data and models temporal trends jointly across multiple dimensions. This approach propagates uncertainty from observations to species-, region-, and cross-regional summaries and allows correlations among redistribution pathways to vary between regions. Recent work has shown that explicitly accounting for uncertainty can substantially shape conclusions about biodiversity status and trends, and our results extend this insight to spatial redistribution under climate change \citep{johnson_revealing_2024}. These methodological differences likely explain why we detect weaker net redistribution signals at cross-regional scales than earlier syntheses.

Climate velocity provides the established currency for the movement expected of species under warming, and concordance between observed shifts and local velocities has been demonstrated in parts of our study domain \citep{burrows_pace_2011, pinsky_marine_2013, gruenburgVerticalClimateVelocity2025}. We instead tracked the mean temperature each population occupied through time, which requires no expectation to be specified for where or how fast a species should move. We additionally compared these trends with those expected under spatially fixed distributions, averaged across species within each region, which gives an approximate indication of how much redistribution buffered background warming. Comparing the shifts estimated here against local climate velocities, both horizontal and vertical, would be a natural extension of this work.

Our estimates describe movement along three physical axes and its thermal consequence, not the whole of how species respond to warming. Species can also moderate their exposure by exploiting fine-scale thermal structure within a location, by altering vertical position or activity between surveys, or through phenological shifts in when they occupy a given area \citep{lawlor_mechanisms_2024, fredston_reimagining_2025, neate-cleggAdvancesBreedingPhenology2024}. Bottom-trawl surveys resolve none of these. They sample the seabed at a small number of fixed times each year, so a change in when a population occupies a location is not separable from a change in how much of it is there; and we assign temperature from a monthly gridded product at approximately 7~km resolution, which averages over the thermal variation individuals actually encounter. Our descriptors are also biomass-weighted means, and are correspondingly insensitive to change confined to range margins, where tracking has been detected in some of these systems \citep{fredston_range_2021}. We analyse populations within survey regions, and species' ranges can extend beyond survey footprints, so our estimates describe distributional change within the sampled domain rather than change in a species' range as a whole; movement across a survey boundary can register as a change in density within it. Statements about redistribution and thermal exposure here therefore refer to the dimensions and the domain we measured, and are likely to understate the compensation available to species overall.

Taken together, our results show that marine species' responses to climate change do not manifest as coherent redistribution that maintains stable thermal conditions. Instead, the temperatures species occupy tend to rise, while redistribution reflects context-dependent responses shaped by local environmental gradients, physical constraints, and species-specific traits and habitat affinities. When aggregated across regions, these heterogeneous trajectories often offset one another, yielding weak net redistribution. Monitoring distributional change alone therefore risks understating the thermal conditions marine species are experiencing, and redistribution and occupied temperature need to be assessed together when evaluating biodiversity responses to continued climate change.

\subsection*{Data sources}

\subsubsection*{Fish observations}
We compiled high-resolution data on fish biomass (kg km\textsuperscript{-2}) from 
a large-scale collection of standardized, fishery-independent bottom-trawl surveys \citep{maureaud_fishglob_data_2024}. Each trawl deployment (haul) recorded the catch by species, with biomass standardized by the sampled (``swept'') area, alongside the haul's location, depth, and time. We selected standardized scientific bottom-trawl surveys spanning at least 15 years, resulting in 17 surveys across 11 regions in the North Atlantic and Northeast Pacific, collectively spanning 1994--2021 (Fig.~\ref{fig:map}). We adopted a 15-year threshold because directional trends in ecological time series are difficult to distinguish from interannual variability over shorter records, typically requiring 10--20 years of observation \citep[e.g.][]{weatherheadFactorsAffectingDetection1998, whiteMinimumTimeRequired2019}. Individual survey time series ranged from 16 to 27 years (median 26 years).
To ensure robust estimates of species' spatial and temporal dynamics, we kept only taxa that: (i) made up 99\% of the cumulative regional biomass, (ii) occurred in at least 15\% of hauls in that region, and (iii) were sampled in at least two hauls per year. These criteria yielded 226 fish populations (species–region combinations) across all regions (Supplementary Data S1).

\subsubsection*{Environmental data}
We obtained bottom temperature data from the Copernicus Global Ocean Physics Reanalysis \citep{european_union-copernicus_marine_service_global_2018}, which provides monthly estimates at 1/12$^{\circ}$ ($\approx$7 km) resolution. While in situ measurements exist for some surveys in certain years, we used this modeled product to obtain temporally averaged and spatially consistent time series. Bathymetric data were derived from the GEBCO 2023 dataset \citep{gebco_bathymetric_compilation_group_2023_gebco_2023_2023}, which provides global seafloor depth at 0.00417° ($\approx$400 m) resolution.

\subsection*{Spatiotemporal modeling}\label{sec:Species spatiotemporal modeling}

\subsubsection*{Model structure} 
We modeled species distributions and their temporal dynamics using spatiotemporal generalized linear mixed-effects models (GLMMs), a flexible class of hierarchical models widely used to quantify spatial and temporal variation in marine species distributions \citep{thorson_geostatistical_2015, thorson_model-based_2016}. For each species (Supplementary Data S1), biomass density was modeled with a Tweedie distribution, using year (as a factor) and log-transformed depth (modeled as a second-order polynomial) as predictors. To account for differences in sampling and seasonal variation we included survey identity and quarter as fixed effects where appropriate. Spatial and spatiotemporal variation was captured through random fields, approximated via the stochastic partial differential equation (SPDE) method with Gaussian Markov random fields \citep{lindgren_explicit_2011}. Models were implemented in R with the \texttt{sdmTMB} package \citep{sdmTMB2025}, which integrates finite-element meshes constructed with \texttt{fmesher} \citep{lindgren_fmesher_2025} into Template Model Builder (TMB) \citep{kristensen_tmb_2016}. Full details on model structure, parameterization, and fitting procedures are provided in Supplementary Appendix A.

\subsubsection*{Prediction grid and environmental matching} 
After model fitting and validation, we predicted species-specific biomass densities on a 4 × 4 km spatial
grid (in local UTM coordinates) covering each region for all modelled years. For each grid cell, we assigned bathymetry and the average bottom temperature over the 12 months preceding the earliest survey month in a region, with temperature values extracted from the gridded product using nearest-neighbor assignment. This procedure generated a consistent spatiotemporal dataset of predicted biomass, depth, and temperature for downstream analyses.

\subsubsection*{Derivation of spatial and thermal metrics}
From the predicted biomass fields described above we computed four annual metrics for each population: the latitudinal and longitudinal centroids of its distribution, its depth centroid, and its mean occupied bottom temperature (Table~\ref{table:derived_quantities}). Centroids were calculated as biomass-weighted means of UTM northing, easting and depth across grid cells \citep{thorson_model-based_2016}, and mean occupied bottom temperature as the biomass-weighted mean of bottom temperature across the same cells. We quantified latitudinal and longitudinal change using centroids rather than range edges, as centroids are less susceptible to noise and provide a more stable measure of overall distributional movement \citep{shoo_detecting_2006}. To isolate the contribution of redistribution to thermal exposure, we additionally computed a static expectation, holding biomass weights constant across years at their grid-cell mean. This represents the temperature each population would have occupied had its distribution remained spatially fixed while ocean temperatures changed.

\subsection*{Bayesian trend analysis}\label{sec:Bayesian trend analysis}

We estimated temporal trends in the derived spatial and thermal metrics using Bayesian mixed-effects models that jointly model multiple, potentially correlated responses. Each response was modeled using a Student-t likelihood to provide robustness to extreme values \citep[e.g.,][]{anderson_black-swan_2017}, with uncertainty from the spatiotemporal modeling propagated via observation-specific standard errors. Correlations among responses were captured through a region-specific multivariate normal (MVN) covariance structure, allowing shared variation among spatial and thermal metrics to be estimated explicitly. Temporal trends were estimated hierarchically as varying slopes, with a global effect of time, region-level deviations, and species-specific deviations nested within regions, enabling inference across biological scales. To improve interpretability and facilitate model fitting, response variables and the time predictor were mean-centered within each species–region combination, such that their means equal zero. Under this parameterization, intercepts were omitted and all coefficients represent deviations from the species–region mean. Time was rescaled to decades, allowing slope parameters to be interpreted directly as rates of change per decade.
Thus the model can be written as:
\begin{gather}
y_{i}^{(j)} \sim \operatorname{Student\text{-}t}
\left(\nu^{(j)}, 
\mu_{i}^{(j)}, 
\sqrt{(\sigma^{(j)})^{2} + (\tau^{(j)}_{i})^{2}}
\right),
\qquad j=1,\dots,4 \label{eq:likelihood}
\\
\mu_{i}^{(j)} =
\beta^{(j)} \cdot \text{decade}_{i}
+ \beta_{\text{reg}[r[i]]}^{(j)} \cdot \text{decade}_{i}
+ \beta_{\text{reg:sp}[r[i],s[i]]}^{(j)} \cdot \text{decade}_{i} \label{eq:linearpred}
\\
\beta_{\text{reg}[r]}^{(j)} \sim 
\operatorname{N}\!\left(0,\sigma_{\text{reg}}^{(j)}\right) \label{eq:regionslope}
\\
\begin{bmatrix}
\beta_{\text{reg:sp}[r,s]}^{(1)} \\
\beta_{\text{reg:sp}[r,s]}^{(2)} \\
\beta_{\text{reg:sp}[r,s]}^{(3)} \\
\beta_{\text{reg:sp}[r,s]}^{(4)}
\end{bmatrix}
\sim 
\operatorname{MVN}\!\left(
\mathbf{0},\,
\mathbf{S}_{r}\,\mathbf{R}_{r}\,\mathbf{S}_{r}
\right),
\qquad \text{for each species}~s~\text{in region}~r \label{eq:mvn}
\\
\mathbf{S}_{r} =
\begin{bmatrix}
\sigma^{(1)}_{\text{reg:sp},r} & 0 & 0 & 0 \\
0 & \sigma^{(2)}_{\text{reg:sp},r} & 0 & 0 \\
0 & 0 & \sigma^{(3)}_{\text{reg:sp},r} & 0 \\
0 & 0 & 0 & \sigma^{(4)}_{\text{reg:sp},r}
\end{bmatrix} \label{eq:sdmatrix}
\\
\mathbf{R}_{r} =
\begin{bmatrix}
1 & \rho_{12,r} & \rho_{13,r} & \rho_{14,r} \\
\rho_{12,r} & 1 & \rho_{23,r} & \rho_{24,r} \\
\rho_{13,r} & \rho_{23,r} & 1 & \rho_{34,r} \\
\rho_{14,r} & \rho_{24,r} & \rho_{34,r} & 1
\end{bmatrix} \label{eq:corrmatrix}
\end{gather}
\noindent Here, each observation $i$ is a single species--region--year combination: one annual value of a given metric for one population ([N] observations in total). Each observation carries a region index $r[i]$ and a species index $s[i]$, and $\mathrm{decade}_i$ denotes its sampling year, mean-centred within the species--region combination and rescaled to decades. The response $y^{(j)}_i$ is the value of metric $j$ for observation $i$, with $j$ indexing the latitudinal centroid, longitudinal centroid, depth centroid and mean occupied bottom temperature (Table~\ref{table:derived_quantities}); $\tau^{(j)}_i$ is the corresponding standard error propagated from the spatiotemporal models, $\sigma^{(j)}$ a response-specific residual scale parameter, and $\nu^{(j)}$ the degrees of freedom. The linear predictor $\mu^{(j)}_i$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:linearpred}) models temporal trends as varying slopes, with a global effect of time $\beta^{(j)}$, region-specific deviations $\beta^{(j)}_{\text{reg}[r]}$, and species-specific deviations nested within region $\beta^{(j)}_{\text{reg:sp}[r,s]}$.
Species-level slope deviations were modelled jointly across responses using a MVN distribution with region-specific covariance matrices $\boldsymbol{\Sigma_r} = \mathbf{S}_{r}\mathbf{R}_{r}\,\mathbf{S}_{r}$. This structure allows temporal trends in different response variables to be correlated among species within the same region, as controlled by $\mathbf{R}_{r}$, while allowing these correlation patterns and the magnitude of response-specific variability to differ among regions.
Priors were weakly informative and guided by published rates of range shifts, depth changes, and ocean warming (Supplementary Appendix B). We fit models in R using the \texttt{brms} package \citep{burkner_brms_2017}, which interfaces with Stan via \texttt{rstan} \citep{stan2024,stan_development_team_rstan_2024}. Supplementary Appendix B Table~S3 maps each term in Eqs.~\ref{eq:likelihood}--\ref{eq:corrmatrix} to the corresponding object in the fitting code. Each model ran with four Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) chains of 4,000 iterations, discarding the first 2,000 as warm-up. The remaining 2,000 samples per chain (8,000 total post–warm-up draws) formed the posterior distribution. Consistency with convergence was confirmed by $\hat{R} < 1.01$, absence of divergent transitions, and effective sample sizes $> 400$ for all key parameters \citep{vehtari_rank-normalization_2021}, as detailed in Supplementary Appendix B.

\subsection*{Regional warming rates and response scaling}
We estimated a temperature trend for each region by fitting a linear regression to annual mean bottom temperature across the regional prediction grid over the study period, expressed per decade (Fig.~\ref{fig:map}). To assess whether regional responses scaled with local warming, we then correlated the region-level posterior median slope for each response with the corresponding regional temperature trend across the eleven regions (Pearson correlation, denoted \textit{r}). These correlations are computed after model fitting, on posterior medians, and are distinct from the within-region correlations among responses estimated inside the model ($\rho$; Eq.~\ref{eq:corrmatrix}), which are posterior parameters.
